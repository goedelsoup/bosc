An impact study answers four questions before a community commits to a project. What does
the project take? What does it give back? Who bears the cost, and for how long? And which of
those numbers is a measurement rather than a promise?

Normally the applicant's engineering consultant writes it. The applicant then files it with
the body that approves the project: a zoning board, a county commission, or a school board
voting on a tax abatement. The study arrives before the vote, because after the vote it
changes nothing.

For the campus at 4110 N. Cole St., no such study appears in the public record this platform
has assembled. The approvals proceeded anyway. Allen County abated 75 % of the real property
tax for 15 years, and it took on perpetual maintenance of the rebuilt corridor roads. The
county's own cost-benefit analysis behind that trade exists, because the county refers to it.
The county withheld it from production.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
  This document is the study that was never filed, rebuilt from documents the public already
  owns. It is assembled after the vote, which is the wrong order. Publishing it is how the
  order gets corrected for the next vote.
</Callout>

## How this study reads evidence

Every figure carries a tag that states what kind of figure it is. A reader never has to guess
whether a number was measured, derived, or asserted.

<ul>
  <li><strong>[verified]</strong> — read directly from a cited document or a public dataset.</li>
  <li><strong>[inference]</strong> — a labelled reading of the assembled record, with its method named.</li>
  <li><strong>[reference]</strong> — an outside published source, cited but not independently checked.</li>
  <li><strong>[open]</strong> — a question the record does not answer, stated as a question.</li>
</ul>

The full evidence grammar, the document pipeline, and the chain-of-custody rules are set out
once for the whole network on the <a href={withBase("/methodology")}>methodology page</a>.

## What this study is not

It is not a permit determination. Where a chapter screens a discharge against a low-flow
statistic, it reproduces the comparison a permit writer would start from. It does not
reproduce the permit decision, which weighs facts this record does not hold.

It is not a forecast. No chapter computes a jobs multiplier, a tax-revenue projection, or a
growth scenario. Those figures require assumptions the record cannot supply, and a study that
supplies them anyway is advocacy wearing a table.

## Why the gaps are published

A conventional study omits what it could not obtain. This one prints it. Each gap states
three things: the finding a real study would report, the specific record needed to compute
it, and the office that holds that record.

That grammar is deliberate. An empty chapter written this way is not a weakness in the study.
It is a measurement of how thin the public record was allowed to become before a
multi-hundred-megawatt project broke ground.
