Every chapter so far has been about something new arriving: a contracted load, a tariff, a zoning
amendment, a corrected denominator. This one is about what was already here. Findlay's defining public
work is not a data center. It is a roughly **$160 million** attempt to stop the Blanchard River from
flooding its downtown — and the town has been paying for it since before anyone in it had heard of a
megawatt hub.

## The spine

- **167 flood-prone properties acquired and demolished since 2007.** `[verified]`
- A **0.25% Hancock County flood-mitigation sales tax**, passed **November 2009**, which "generated
  more than $32,000,000 over a ten-year period." `[verified]`
- A **2016 Memorandum of Agreement** between the county commissioners and the **Maumee Watershed
  Conservancy District**, authorizing MWCD "to identify opportunities for flood risk reduction."
  `[verified]` (Its exact execution date is `[open]`.)
- Engineer of record: **Stantec**. A 2018 benefit-cost analysis modeled construction at
  **$154,756,000** and a program **benefit-cost ratio of 2.94** — $484.3M of benefits against $165.0M
  of costs. `[verified]`

<div class="callout">
  <p class="callout-title">MWCD is not MWCD</p>
  <p>
    The administering body is the <strong>Maumee</strong> Watershed Conservancy District — northwest
    Ohio, the Maumee/Lake Erie basin, offices in Defiance, organized under ORC Chapter 6101, governed
    by a Conservancy Court composed of the Common Pleas judges of its fifteen member counties. It is
    <em>not</em> the <strong>Muskingum</strong> Watershed Conservancy District of eastern Ohio, which
    shares the initialism and is a different entity with a different court and a different basin.
    Every regional-authority story in Ohio has one of these collisions in it.
  </p>
</div>

> **The skill:** read a program as a chain of *instruments*, not as a total. A headline number
> ("$160 million") is a planning estimate. An obligation letter, a capital-budget line, a grant award,
> and a sales-tax levy are four different legal objects with four different degrees of certainty and
> four different ways of falling through. Listing them separately is what lets you say which parts are
> actually committed.

## The four projects

**Phase 1 benching is complete** (2024): floodplain benching near Swale Park, removal of four low-head
dams downstream of Lye Creek, and instream riffle construction. On **2025-09-18** the City, the county
commissioners and Stantec submitted a **Letter of Map Revision** to FEMA on the finished work —
**375 parcels (~15 acres) completely removed** from the Special Flood Hazard Area, and **985 parcels
(~67 acres) remaining in it but at a reduced 100-year water-surface elevation.** FEMA's Letter of Final
Determination makes that official; no effective date is set. `[open]` The Phase 1 construction
contractor and price are `[open]` — not disclosed in any reachable source.

**The Eagle Creek Dry Storage Basin** is the cornerstone: an **~765-acre** dry impoundment in Eagle
Township about four miles south of downtown, bounded by Township Road 76 and US-68. Final design is
complete, property acquisition is complete, and the ODNR Dam Safety construction permit is in hand.
Modeled effect on the 1% annual-chance event: a **~2,550 cfs (16%) peak-flow reduction**, **~2.2 feet**
of base-flood-elevation lowering near the Blanchard/Eagle Creek confluence downtown, and **~1,830
parcels / 1,400 acres** out of the regulatory floodplain. `[verified]`

**Phase 2 downtown benching** was preceded by a soil remediation (December 2025 – February 2026) that
removed approximately **7,325 tons of soil** contaminated with lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium and
PAHs from historic industrial use, plus 1,070 tons of glass fill and 432 tons of asphalt. `[reference]`

**A Norfolk Southern bridge** more than a century old is to be replaced, widening the span from 150 to
300 feet to pass flood flow — estimated at ~$16.7M, expected to lower flood levels about 0.3 ft at Main
Street in a 100-year storm. `[verified]`

## The discipline: an apparent low bid is not an award

Both cornerstone contracts opened bids on **2026-06-30**:

| project | apparent low bidder | bid | engineer's estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eagle Creek Dry Storage Basin | Miller Brothers Construction | $32,075,000 | $42,376,000 |
| Phase 2 / Additional Hydraulic Improvements | Helms & Sons | $3,699,040 | $6,170,000 |

The MWCD board was to consider award recommendations at its 9:00 a.m. meeting on **2026-07-14** in
Defiance; the Norfolk Southern bid opening was extended to the same date in Atlanta. **As of this
reading neither outcome is published.** `[open]`

<div class="callout callout--open">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not upgrade "apparent low" to "awarded"</p>
  <p>
    A bid tabulation records who submitted the lowest number on the day. An award is a board vote, and
    boards reject bids, rebid scopes, and negotiate. Two contracts worth $35.8 million sit in that gap
    right now, tracked as a dated lead against the board minutes that will close it.
  </p>
</div>

## The two threads still open under it

**Eminent domain.** An eminent-domain firm's writeup describes two private property owners contesting
a taking for the flood-control basins, arguing MWCD "did not have the right to take the land and did
not follow eminent domain procedures according to Ohio state law," with the case unresolved.
`[reference]` Landowner names, the court, the case number and the filing date are all unpublished —
and the writeup references an older three-basin plan and never names Eagle Creek, so **even the
identification with the Eagle Creek appropriation is `[inference]`.**

**Who pays to run it.** The program FAQ answers the assessment question verbatim: "The annual
operation and maintenance expenses are estimated at $100,000. The method for covering this expense has
yet to be finalized but may include funding through grants, the City of Findlay, the County, or
assessments." `[verified]` So an assessment is **one of four listed possibilities, not an enacted
instrument** — and the Hancock County "Proposed Assessments" document that turns up in a search is the
**Howard Run petition ditch**, an ORC Chapter 6131 county ditch improvement administered by the
commissioners. Same statutory family, different instrument, different body. Do not conflate them.

## What you've established — and where it lands

Findlay has spent nearly two decades and nine figures on the Blanchard, funded by its own sales tax,
the state capital budget, FEMA, and the federal transportation department, and administered by a
fifteen-county conservancy district. Two contracts are bid and unawarded. One taking is contested by
persons the record does not name.

And it runs downstream. Thirty-odd river miles below the outfall of Chapter 4 sits **Ottawa**, another
point in this network — which drinks and discharges the same water, and whose only new flood relief is
derived entirely from work done up here. That is the shape worth holding on to as the load arrives:
this river is already a shared, expensive, litigated public asset, and every chapter of this story is
about a different claim on it.
