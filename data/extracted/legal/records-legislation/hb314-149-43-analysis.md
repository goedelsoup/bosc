# Sub. H.B. 314 (136th G.A.) — the public-records bill aimed at requesters like this one

**Source:** `data/documents/legal/records-legislation/hb314_02_PH.pdf` — *Sub. H.B. No. 314, As Passed by the House*, 48 pp. Structured read: [`hb314-149-43.digest.yaml`](hb314-149-43.digest.yaml).

> Analysis, not legal advice. Provision reads trace to the cited division/page of the bill; verify exact new-vs-existing wording against the source PDF and against current R.C. 149.43 before quoting in a filing. This is the **As-Passed-by-the-House** version — not enacted law.

## What it is

A substitute bill (sponsors Reps. **Isaacsohn** and **Ray**) that amends **R.C. 149.43**, the Public Records Act, and repeals the existing section (Section 2 — the standard amendatory form). It does two things, both new, both in **division (B)**:

1. **Arms the public office with an affirmative lawsuit** against a requester it deems harassing or disruptive `[verified: (B)(2)(c)]`.
2. **Legitimizes billing the requester for a private contractor** to answer a "voluminous" request `[verified: (B)(6)(b)]`.

Everything the corpus's mandamus posture relies on in **division (C)** — the $100/business-day-to-$1,000 statutory-damages engine, injury "conclusively presumed," and the writ path itself — is left **intact** `[verified: (C)(3), (C)(1)(b)]`. So HB 314 is not a change to the *remedy* the relator pursues; it is a new set of *front-end* defenses and cost levers an office can raise against the request in the first place.

## The harass/disrupt action — new (B)(2)(c)

Where a requester submits **multiple related or unrelated requests** to the same office and, "based on the **volume or the repeated nature**" of them, the office "has reason to believe" they are "intended to **harass** … or to **disrupt the essential functions**" of the office, the office may **sue the requester** in the common pleas court of its own county to limit or deny the requests `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(i)]`.

The mechanics that matter:

- **It tolls the office's own duty to respond.** "While the action or any appeal … is pending, the requirement that the public office … respond to the requests … is tolled." `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(ii)]`
- **The court can bar future requests for up to five years** if it finds the requester likely to keep filing harassing/disrupting requests; violating that order can extend it; requesting **anonymously to circumvent** the order draws a **fine up to $100/violation** `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(iii)]`.
- **The requester's only fee recovery is discretionary and defensive.** If the court finds the requests were **not** intended to harass/disrupt, it *may* award the requester reasonable attorney's fees and expenses **against the office that sued** — "may," not "shall," and subject to R.C. 9.86 `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(iv)]`.
- **The exemptions are narrow** `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(v)]`: a **journalist** (as defined in (B)(9)(c)); a **pro se litigant** requesting records "pertaining to litigation in which the pro se litigant is a **named party**"; and government requesters (state agencies, public colleges, public defenders, government attorneys). Listed requesters cannot have access restricted under this division `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(vi)]`, and an office may demand documentary proof of exempt status under (B)(11) before suing a suspected public defender.

## The contractor-fee route — new (B)(6)(b)

If a request is "**voluminous** and it is therefore reasonable to do so," the office may **engage a private contractor** to respond — expressly **including making required redactions** — and may fold the contractor's services into the **cost to produce** `[verified: (B)(6)(b)(i)]`. Guardrails: the contractor cost may **not** push the fee above the highest permissible fee in the existing fee structure `[verified: (B)(6)(b)(i)]`; the contractor must dispose of copies, return originals, not profit from the records, and not disclose redacted content `[verified: (B)(6)(b)(ii)]`. "Hours to produce" (B)(23) now counts contractor hours, and — critically for a mandamus — a court **shall not consider whether a contractor would have produced records faster** when judging promptness `[verified: bill body ≈ printed p. 23]`.

## The three things that matter for this corpus

**1. Tolling is the real weapon, and it points straight at the mandamus theory.** The [mandamus analysis](../../../../docs/legal/mandamus-analysis.md) builds the live claim on "unreasonable delay = constructive denial" under §149.43(B) — the violation that accrues (C)(3) damages. Under HB 314, an office facing a voluminous requester can **file the (B)(2)(c) action and toll its own response duty** for the life of the case and any appeal `[verified: (B)(2)(c)(ii)]`. That converts delay from a liability the requester monetizes into a court-sanctioned stay the office initiates. `[inference]` The (C)(3) damages engine survives on paper, but the event that triggers it — an unmet (B) obligation — can be suspended by the office's own filing.

**2. The relator is not clearly exempt.** `[open]` Neither safe harbor plainly covers this program. The **pro se** exemption is keyed to records "pertaining to litigation in which the pro se litigant is a **named party**" — it reaches the records at issue in the relator's own PRR mandamus, but not the broader research corpus (Shawnee II, CRA/ROI, corridor, hydrology). The **journalist** exemption ((B)(9)(c): a person "engaged in … disseminating information for the general public" through a "news medium") is a *plausible* fit given the published investigative series — but it is untested and turns on whether the Watermark Directory reads as a "news medium." This is the exposure to resolve deliberately, not assume away.

**3. Cost becomes a breadth lever.** `[inference]` An office can now bill the relator for a contractor's review/redaction of a voluminous request (capped at the statutory per-page fee), and the mandamus court may not credit the office for *declining* to use a contractor to go faster `[verified: (B)(6)(b), printed p. 23]`. The practical counter is the same discipline the corpus already applies — **narrow, well-scoped, itemized requests** that are hard to characterize as "voluminous" or "harassing," and a written record that each request maps to a specific documented question.

## What the committee substitute changed (As Introduced → As Passed)

Both versions are in the corpus: `hb314_00_IN.pdf` (As Introduced, 42 pp, no cosponsors) and `hb314_02_PH.pdf` (Sub., As Passed, 48 pp, 6 cosponsors). A textual diff shows the committee did not soften the core design — the two new mechanisms and the response-**tolling** were in the bill from introduction — but it **balanced** it at the edges `[verified: diff of the two PDFs]`:

- **The prospective ban got a ceiling.** Introduced, a future-request order "remains in force **indefinitely** unless the order provides for its expiration." As passed, it runs "for a period of **up to five years**" (extendable on violation) `[(B)(2)(c)(iii)]`. An open-ended ban became a capped one — the single most requester-protective change.
- **A requester fee-shift was added.** Introduced gave a requester who *defeated* the office's action **nothing**. The substitute inserted **new (B)(2)(c)(iv)**: if the court finds the requests were not harassing/disrupting, it *may* award the requester attorney's fees and litigation expenses against the office (subject to R.C. 9.86) `[verified]`. That new clause is why the exemptions list renumbered from introduced (iv) to passed (v).
- **Anti-circumvention teeth were added.** The **$100/violation** fine for requesting anonymously to circumvent an order is new in the substitute `[(B)(2)(c)(iii)]`.
- **The contractor route gained a fee cap and confidentiality duties.** Introduced let the office fold contractor cost into the fee with no stated ceiling. The substitute added the **highest-permissible-fee cap** and **new (B)(6)(b)(ii)** — the contractor must dispose of copies, return originals, not profit from the records, and not disclose redacted content `[verified]`.

Net: the committee made the harass/disrupt action more balanced for a requester who is ultimately vindicated, while hardening it against evasion — but the lever that matters most here, **response-tolling while the office's own action is pending, was in both versions and survived**. `[inference]`
